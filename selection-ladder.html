selection_score

_hindcast_score() · curation/__init__.py:396

At forecast time you hold two things: a history, and a horizon to fill. You do not hold the answer. So any rule for choosing among 98 methods has to be computable from history alone — that constraint is what selection_score exists to satisfy.

The trick is to manufacture a question you can mark. Hide the last stretch of the history, forecast it as if it were the future, and compare against the values you hid. Nothing withheld is touched; the method is graded on data it already had, held back on purpose.

The procedure

  1. Set fold_horizon = min(selection_horizon, len(history) // (folds+1)) — 8 by default.
  2. For fold i, cut at validation_end = len(history) − i·fold_horizon and train_end = validation_end − fold_horizon.
  3. Fit on history[:train_end], forecast fold_horizon steps, score against history[train_end:validation_end].
  4. Step the origin back and repeat, selection_folds = 3 times.
  5. selection_score = fmean(errors).

Three origins rather than one because a single split is a single sample: a method can win one window on luck. Averaging across receding origins is a cheap variance reduction, and it is why this is rolling-origin backtesting rather than a plain holdout.

What it is not

It is not an estimate of how accurate the method will be. Its absolute value is close to meaningless — the folds are 8 steps, the real task may be 24 or 2 798. It is used for one purpose only: to rank methods against each other. Only the ordering is consumed, by a single min().

The assumption it rests on

That the ranking is stable across origins — that whichever method did best on the last few chunks will do best on the next one. Your own catalog states it in rolling_origin_champion_selector: “historical forecast-origin errors rank candidate methods similarly to the next origin,” failing when “a regime change after the last validation origin makes the historical champion obsolete.”

Guards

  • A fold that raises, returns the wrong length, or emits a non-finite value is skipped, not scored.
  • Fewer than ceil(available_folds × 0.8) surviving folds → returns None → the method is unselectable. Better absent than ranked on one lucky window.
  • Ties break on method_id, so routing is deterministic.
  • If nothing scores, fall back to the alphabetically first success — arbitrary, but better than eating the 1000.0 penalty.
Cost. Every method runs 1 + folds times per task — the real forecast plus three auditions. At 98 methods × 80 tasks that is roughly 31 000 forecasts, which is why a cheap classical method and a foundation model are not interchangeable here.

oracle_score — better called the ceiling

min(by_item_errors[task]) · curation/__init__.py:566

Same shape, one word different:

routed = fmean( error of the selected method, per task ) ceiling = fmean( error of the best method, per task )

To know which method was best you must first know the answer. So the ceiling is computed after the truth is revealed, by looking at every method's real error and taking the smallest. It is not a strategy anyone can run. It is a bound.

Why the word “oracle”

From complexity theory, where an oracle is a black box that answers some hard question for free, used to reason about what a machine could do if that part were solved. Here the free answer is “which method wins on this task.” The name describes the cheating, not the method — which is exactly why ceiling is the clearer word, and what this page uses.

It is not “the best single method”

A common misreading. The ceiling is allowed to change its mind per task; a single method is not. So the ceiling is always at least as good as the best fixed method, usually better:

full98, 98 methods, per-series-type: best single method (timesfm_2_5) 0.278 ceiling, switching per type 0.248

That 0.030 is what switching itself is worth — before any question of whether a real router can capture it.

Why compute a number nobody can reach

Because one bad score has two possible causes, and the pair separates them. On its own, dictionary_score = 0.583 tells you the module is weak but not why:

  • Ceiling poor. No method in the pool can handle these tasks. Routing is irrelevant — a perfect router still picks from what is there. Fix the pool.
  • Ceiling good, gap wide. The right answer was present on most tasks and routing kept missing it. Adding methods is wasted effort; the loss is entirely in selection. Fix the router.

Those are opposite prescriptions, and you cannot tell them apart from the routed score alone. That is the whole reason the diagnostic is worth its cost.

The gap is not a target. No realizable router closes it — some tasks are genuinely ambiguous from history alone, as task_186 shows. Treat it as headroom, and treat a shrinking gap across generations as the signal that routing is improving.
